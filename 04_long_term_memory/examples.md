# 🔬 Long-Term Memory — Real-World Examples

## Extraordinary Memory Feats

### 1. Solomon Shereshevsky ("S") — The Man Who Couldn't Forget
- Studied by Luria for 30 years (1920s–1960s)
- Could recall lists of 70+ items years later without error
- Used synesthesia: Every number had color, texture, taste
- **The cost**: Couldn't abstract, couldn't recognize faces (too many details)
- Proves: "Perfect memory" is not always advantageous

### 2. Kim Peek (Inspiration for "Rain Man")
- Memorized 12,000+ books verbatim
- Could read two pages simultaneously (one per eye)
- **Mechanism**: Absent corpus callosum → unusual neural organization
- **Trade-off**: Couldn't button his own shirt (motor/procedural memory poor)

### 3. Memory Athletes (World Memory Championships)
- Memorize: 500+ digits in 5 minutes, deck of cards in <15 seconds
- **NOT** born with special brains — use learned techniques (Method of Loci)
- fMRI shows: Spatial navigation areas (hippocampus!) activated during memorization
- Proves: LTM technique, not hardware, determines exceptional performance

## Everyday LTM Demonstrations

### 4. The Baker/baker Effect
- "Remember, his name is Baker" vs. "Remember, he is a baker"
- Same word, different encoding depth
- Profession → rich associations (flour, oven, apron, early morning)
- Name → shallow encoding (just a label, no hooks)
- Proves: Depth of processing determines LTM encoding strength

### 5. The Google Effect (Digital Amnesia)
- People remember WHERE to find info (which folder, which app) better than the info itself
- Knowing "I can look it up" reduces encoding effort
- Your brain treats the internet as external memory (transactive memory)

### 6. Childhood Amnesia
- Adults can't recall events before age 3-4
- NOT because they weren't encoded — infants DO form memories
- Problem: Hippocampus isn't mature enough for durable episodic encoding
- Also: Language framework for "stories" isn't developed yet (can't narrativize)

## Memory Duration Records

### 7. Bahrick's Permastore Study (1984)
- Tested Spanish vocabulary retention over 50 YEARS (people who studied in school)
- Pattern: Rapid forgetting for 3-6 years → then PLATEAU
- What survived 50 years: ~60% of well-learned vocabulary
- Named it "permastore" — once fully consolidated, some memories never fade

### 8. Flashbulb Memories (Brown & Kulik, 1977)
- "Where were you when you heard about 9/11?"
- Vivid, confident, seemingly photographic recall
- BUT: Research shows they're just as INACCURATE as normal memories
- Amygdala boosts CONFIDENCE and VIVIDNESS, not fidelity
- You feel certain about details that never happened

## Failure to Encode vs. Failure to Retrieve

### 9. Tip-of-the-Tongue (TOT) Phenomenon
- You KNOW the word, can feel its shape, maybe its first letter
- Proves the memory EXISTS (not lost) but retrieval is temporarily blocked
- Usually resolves spontaneously (the right neural pathway eventually activates)
- Frequency increases with age (retrieval slows, not storage)

### 10. Context-Dependent Memory (Godden & Baddeley, 1975)
- Divers learned word lists underwater or on land
- Recall was 50% better when TEST context matched STUDY context
- Your environment is part of the retrieval cue
- Exam tip: Study in similar conditions to where you'll be tested
